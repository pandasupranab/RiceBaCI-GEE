## Abstract

Phenology metrics derived from Sentinel-2 time series — start of season (SOS), peak of season (POS), and end of season (EOS) — are increasingly used as outcome variables in causal impact studies of tropical cyclones, drought, and heat stress on rice cultivation. The reliability of these metrics under standard double-logistic fitting pipelines has not been systematically audited. We document a reproducible failure mode — day-of-year (DOY) quantisation at fitting-window boundaries — that produces artefactual phenological signals indistinguishable from genuine climatic effects. Using 8 districts × 6 years of Sentinel-2 Level-2A (L2A) 10-m normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) dekadal composites across coastal and inland Odisha, India, we show that the pre-quality-control (pre-QC) (v1.0.2) pipeline concentrates 72.7% of district-year EOS estimates at a single boundary DOY (349) and 65.6% of POS estimates at a single DOY (288); after the three-gate QC framework is applied (v2.0), the POS and EOS mode-shares both collapse to 8.3% and the SOS mode-share falls from 20.3% to 12.5%, all three comfortably below the Gate A threshold of 0.20. We then apply a two-way fixed-effects difference-in-differences (DiD) design — at both district-median (Model 1) and pixel (Model 2) resolution — using five coastal treatment districts, three inland controls, and three cyclones (Fani 2019, Amphan 2020, Yaas 2021). All three phenometrics return null effects after QC (τ_SOS = +7.6 d, τ_POS = −2.3 d, τ_EOS = −4.1 d; all wild-cluster bootstrap p-values, p_WCB > 0.38), and the null is robust to parallel-trends, missing-at-random (MAR), and Manski/Lee bounds diagnostics. The QC framework eliminates boundary artefacts that would otherwise generate false positives. Full pipeline released as RiceBaCI-GEE v2.0 (Zenodo: 10.5281/zenodo.20587316; OSF: 10.17605/OSF.IO/C4MP8).

**Keywords:** Sentinel-2; rice phenology; quality control; double-logistic fitting; phenometric artefacts; difference-in-differences; coastal Odisha; cyclone impact

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Satellite phenology as an outcome variable in causal climate studies

Rice (*Oryza sativa* L.) cultivation across coastal South and Southeast Asia is increasingly monitored through satellite-derived phenology metrics. The launch of Sentinel-2A in June 2015, followed by Sentinel-2B in March 2017, completed a dual-satellite constellation that opened a new era for field-scale phenology monitoring: with 10-m spatial resolution in the red and near-infrared bands, a 5-day revisit under dual-satellite operations, and a globally harmonised Level-2A surface-reflectance archive (`COPERNICUS/S2_SR_HARMONIZED`) available from 2017 onward, Sentinel-2 NDVI time series support field- to district-scale assessments of crop sowing dates, canopy development, and harvest timing (Vrieling et al., 2018; Meroni et al., 2021).

These phenological transition dates — start of season (SOS), peak of season (POS), and end of season (EOS) — have moved beyond descriptive characterisation into the role of outcome variables in quasi-experimental impact studies. Studies using phenology as an outcome include characterisation of drought- and heat-stress effects on growing-season timing (Vrieling et al., 2018; Meroni et al., 2021) and mapping of rice cropping systems in cloud-prone monsoon regions where season-timing extraction is itself a primary deliverable (Singha et al., 2019). The underlying logic is straightforward: if an extreme event (cyclone, drought, heat wave) causally disrupts crop development, that disruption should manifest as a detectable shift in phenometric timing relative to an unaffected control group, and the magnitude of that shift is, in turn, informative about yield outcomes through documented phenology–yield relationships, although the mapping is statistical rather than deterministic and varies by cultivar and management regime.

The methodological appeal of phenometric outcomes is strong. Unlike officially reported yield data — which are released annually, subject to administrative aggregation, and confounded by management responses — satellite phenometrics are derived from dense, spatially granular NDVI time series that are observed directly at the field scale across treatment and control units within the same season. Phenometrics do not preclude farmer adaptation: adaptive transplanting and replanting decisions are themselves visible in the SOS signal and are a substantive part of what the outcome captures. Difference-in-differences (DiD) and before-after-control-impact (BACI) designs that exploit the staggered timing of extreme events are therefore a natural candidate framework for phenometric outcomes, and the present paper develops and evaluates one such application for cyclone-driven rice phenology shifts in coastal Odisha.

### 1.2 The unexamined reliability of phenometric extraction pipelines

Despite this growing methodological dependence, the reliability of phenometric extraction pipelines has not been systematically audited in the context of impact studies. Published work on phenology extraction uncertainty has focused primarily on sensor-specific and algorithmic issues — logistic-fitting performance on noisy NDVI/EVI time series (Cao et al., 2015), smoothing-parameter and curve-fitting sensitivity (Beck et al., 2006; Jönsson & Eklundh, 2004; Forkel et al., 2015), and the relationship between satellite-derived phenometrics and field-level crop variables (Bolton & Friedl, 2013). These are important concerns, but they address the accuracy of individual pixel-year estimates rather than the distributional properties of phenometric outputs that determine whether a DiD estimator can recover an unbiased treatment effect.

A distinct and under-examined class of errors arises from the interaction between double-logistic fitting algorithms and the finite temporal boundaries of the fitting window. When a Kharif rice pixel has an NDVI trajectory whose senescence limb reaches the fitting-window edge (typically the last dekadal bin near DOY 349 in standard GEE implementations for a November-end Kharif window), the double-logistic optimiser assigns the EOS date at the boundary DOY rather than extrapolating beyond the observed data. This produces a hard spike in the empirical distribution of EOS values at the window-edge DOY: a quantisation artefact that is entirely an artefact of the fitting window and carries no information about true agronomic end-of-season timing. An analogous artefact at the peak of the canopy trajectory can concentrate POS estimates at a single dekadal bin when the smoothing-and-fitting combination flattens the green-up plateau. If either artefact is systematically more prevalent in one group (e.g., treatment districts with cyclone-damaged crops that senesce later) than another, the resulting DiD coefficient will be biased — potentially producing a spuriously significant treatment effect.

The broader issue of fitting-window edge handling in vegetation phenology products is acknowledged in the documentation of operational MODIS land surface phenology datasets (Gray et al., 2019), but the implications for causal inference on short panels at district scale have not been explored. The quantisation concern is amplified in multi-year panels, where year-to-year changes in the mode structure of phenometric distributions can be conflated with genuine climatic or agronomic signals.

### 1.3 Contribution of this study

This paper makes three primary contributions. First, we document and characterise DOY quantisation artefacts in Sentinel-2 Kharif rice phenology extraction over coastal Odisha, demonstrating that the EOS distribution in a standard pipeline carries a mode-share of 72.7% at a single boundary DOY (349), the POS distribution carries a mode-share of 65.6% at DOY 288, and the SOS distribution carries a mode-share of 20.3% at DOY 196. After the QC framework is applied, the POS and EOS mode-shares collapse to 8.3% and the SOS mode-share falls to 12.5%, with the modal DOYs shifting away from the boundary-adjacent dates (EOS 349→354/329 tie, POS 288→274/290/293 tie, SOS 196→178). This provides a clear empirical demonstration of how fitting-window boundary effects and dekadal-bin quantisation can concentrate phenometric values and distort impact estimates.

Second, we propose a three-gate reproducible QC framework for Sentinel-2 phenometric panels used in causal impact studies. The framework consists of: (i) Gate A — mode-share thresholds on the empirical distributions of SOS, POS, and EOS to flag boundary-contaminated panels; (ii) Gate B — biological plausibility checks tied to the known Kharif crop calendar, rejecting phenometric values outside agronomically defensible windows; and (iii) Gate C — fit-quality screening using the root-mean-squared residual from the double-logistic fit, with an RMSE-based threshold that excludes cells where the optimiser failed to converge or returned a poor fit to the underlying NDVI trajectory. The framework is parameterised from Odisha Kharif rice agronomy but is designed to be transferable to other tropical rice systems with straightforward calendar adaptation.

Third, we apply the QC-cleaned panel in a difference-in-differences case study of cyclone impacts on Kharif rice phenology in coastal Odisha, using three inland districts as controls and three cyclone events (Fani 2019, Amphan 2020, Yaas 2021) as treatment variation. The DiD is estimated at two resolutions: a district-median specification (Model 1, n = 48 district-years) and a pixel-level specification (Model 2, n = 1,223 QC-passing pixel-years). All three phenometrics return null effects after QC at the district scale (all wild-cluster bootstrap p-values between 0.39 and 0.47), and the pixel-level estimates are likewise null at conventional thresholds (smallest p = 0.096 for POS). The null is robust to parallel-trends, MAR vs. MNAR, and Manski/Lee selection-bound diagnostics. We frame the null as a validation outcome consistent with the QC framework's intent: by removing the boundary-quantisation variance that drives the v1.0.2 mode-share spikes — the same variance that would mechanically generate spurious treatment effects under any treatment–control split of an artefact-contaminated panel — the QC framework leaves a panel in which the cyclone DiD effect, if any, is not detectable at the design's minimum-detectable-effect threshold.

### 1.4 Relationship to existing literature

The methodological positioning of this paper is at the intersection of three literature streams. The first is the satellite phenology methods literature, where smoothing-parameter and curve-fitting sensitivity have been addressed by Jönsson and Eklundh (2004), Eilers (2003), and Forkel et al. (2015), but not in the specific context of impact-study panel construction. The second is the remote sensing data-quality literature, where cross-sensor and within-product comparisons of phenometric outputs (Meroni et al., 2021; Beck et al., 2006) provide a precedent for systematic quality assessment before downstream analysis, although these comparisons do not yet extend to causal-inference-grade panel screening. The third is the causal inference in climate-agriculture literature, where DiD designs are increasingly applied to agricultural outcomes (Burke and Emerick, 2016) under modern guidance on staggered-adoption identification (Goodman-Bacon, 2021), but where methodological guidance on pre-DiD panel quality assurance for satellite-derived outcome variables remains absent.

We do not claim that cyclones have no effect on rice phenology. Our null result at the 8-district, 3-cyclone-event scale is consistent with several mechanisms that the present design is not well-powered to resolve: the three cyclones varied substantially in track, intensity, and storm-surge footprint, so the pooled treat × post effect averages over heterogeneous treatment intensities; the panel-level minimum-detectable effect (~22.7 d for SOS, 8.1 d for POS, and 12.9 d for EOS at 80% power; §4.1, Table S8) is large relative to plausible cyclone-induced shifts in transplanting and harvest timing; and within-district surge exposure is itself heterogeneous, although the pixel-level specification (Model 2, n = 1,223) helps absorb the part of this heterogeneity that is spatially resolvable in QC-passing rice pixels. The selection-bias diagnostics (MAR vs. MNAR test, Table S5; Manski/Lee bounds, Table S6) rule out QC-induced selection on treatment as a plausible driver of the null. The contribution of this paper is therefore not a definitive assessment of cyclone–phenology relationships, but rather a reproducible toolkit that allows future studies to conduct such assessments with phenometric panels free of known artefacts.

## 2. Study Area and Data

### 2.1 Study area

The study area encompasses eight districts of Odisha state, eastern India, divided into two groups: five coastal treatment districts and three inland control districts (Figure 1). The five coastal treatment districts — Baleshwar (BLS), Bhadrak (BHA), Kendrapara (KDP), Jagatsinghpur (JGS), and Puri (PUR) — form a contiguous coastal strip extending approximately 480 km along the northern Bay of Bengal shoreline, from the Subarnarekha River estuary in the north to Chilika Lake in the south. Combined, they cover approximately 12,389 km² of which roughly 50% is cropland under the European Space Agency (ESA) WorldCover v200 classification (Zanaga et al., 2022). Kharif rice is the dominant crop within this cropland fraction; published district-level statistics for these five districts report a combined Kharif paddy area on the order of 0.6–0.8 million hectares in a normal season, with Kharif rice (locally termed Sali rice) accounting for the majority of cropped area.

The three inland control districts — Angul (ANG), Dhenkanal (DHK), and Cuttack (CTK) — are situated in the Mahanadi basin, more than 80 km from the nearest treatment landfall, and are categorically unexposed to Bay of Bengal storm surge. They share the same South-West monsoon rainfall climatology as the coastal districts and maintain comparable Kharif rice cropping systems, making them appropriate counterfactual units for a difference-in-differences design. Cuttack is the closest inland district to the coast and serves as a sensitivity anchor; the inland-control identification strategy is examined further through the cell-level robustness analysis in Supplement S4.

The climate across all eight districts is classified as sub-tropical humid (Köppen Aw), with a South-West monsoon season from June to September that accounts for approximately 80% of annual precipitation. Mean annual rainfall ranges from approximately 1,400 mm in Puri district in the south to over 1,800 mm in Baleshwar in the north. The dominant rice cropping system is transplanted Kharif rice (locally termed Sali rice), with transplanting typically occurring from mid-June to early August, heading in September–October, and harvest in October–November.

The phenometric extraction is performed on aggregated analysis cells nested within the WorldCover class 40 (cropland) mask; total cell counts per district range from 33 (Jagatsinghpur) to 107 (Angul) for a panel total of 520 cells across the 8 districts (Table 1). The total estimation panel is 48 district-year observations (8 districts × 6 years: 2019–2024), yielding 15 treatment × post observations (5 coastal districts × 3 cyclone years).

### 2.2 Cyclone events

Three major tropical cyclones made landfall along or near the coastal Odisha study area within three consecutive pre-Kharif seasons, providing the treatment variation for the DiD design.

**Cyclone Fani (3 May 2019)** was an Extremely Severe Cyclonic Storm (ESCS) that made landfall near Puri with peak 3-min sustained winds of approximately 215 km h⁻¹ at peak intensity and approximately 185 km h⁻¹ at landfall, accompanied by a storm surge of 1.0–1.5 m along the Puri–Kendrapara coastline (Knapp et al., 2010; India Meteorological Department (IMD) post-landfall report). Fani is one of the most intense cyclones to have made landfall in Odisha in the modern observational record and caused widespread inundation and agricultural damage across all five treatment districts.

**Cyclone Amphan (20 May 2020)** was a Super Cyclonic Storm (SuCS) that made primary landfall on the Sundarbans coast of West Bengal but produced significant surge and rainfall in Baleshwar and Bhadrak districts in northern Odisha. Peak winds at landfall were approximately 155–165 km h⁻¹ with a surge height of 1.5–2.0 m (Knapp et al., 2010).

**Cyclone Yaas (26 May 2021)** was a Very Severe Cyclonic Storm (VSCS) that made landfall near Baleshwar, Odisha, with peak winds of approximately 140 km h⁻¹ (gusting to 155 km h⁻¹) and a storm surge of 1.0–2.0 m inundating Baleshwar and Bhadrak districts (Knapp et al., 2010).

All three events made landfall between 3 May and 26 May, four to eight weeks before the typical Kharif transplanting window (mid-June to early August). Cyclone track data and intensity time series were obtained from the NOAA/NCEI IBTrACS v04r01 archive (Knapp et al., 2010; https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/international-best-track-archive). The post-monsoon seasons of 2022, 2023, and 2024 form the control period in the panel; no IBTrACS-catalogued cyclone track penetrated within 100 km of the study area during these seasons. Supplement S2 (Figure S1) places the 2019–2021 treatment cluster in the full 1990–2024 IBTrACS climatology of pre-Kharif Bay of Bengal landfalls and documents the formal Poisson test (p ≈ 0.04) under which the three consecutive landfalls within a six-year window are statistically unusual at approximately the 5% level.

### 2.3 Satellite data

All satellite and ancillary datasets were accessed via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) cloud computing platform (Gorelick et al., 2017). The four data sources used are described in the four sub-paragraphs that follow.

**Sentinel-2 L2A.** Surface reflectance data from the harmonised Sentinel-2 (S2) Level-2A (L2A) collection (Google Earth Engine collection ID: `COPERNICUS/S2_SR_HARMONIZED`) were used for NDVI time series construction. The harmonised collection applies processing-baseline corrections to ensure radiometric consistency across the full 2017–2024 archive. Cloud and cloud-shadow pixels were masked using the Sentinel-2 Scene Classification Layer (SCL); only pixels classified as vegetation (SCL 4) or bare soil (SCL 5) were retained for cropland NDVI compositing, with all cloud, shadow, snow, water, and saturated classes flagged as invalid. NDVI was computed as (B8 − B4)/(B8 + B4) per valid observation.

**ESA WorldCover v200.** The 10-m global land cover product (Zanaga et al., 2022; GEE: `ESA/WorldCover/v200`) was used to restrict analysis to cropland pixels (class 40). All non-cropland pixels were excluded from phenometric extraction to prevent contamination of district medians by non-agricultural vegetation signals.

**IBTrACS v04r01.** Cyclone track, intensity, and landfall data for Fani, Amphan, and Yaas were obtained from the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (Knapp et al., 2010). A 50-km IBTrACS radius buffer was used to define the spatial extent of cyclone exposure in the treatment allocation; all five coastal treatment districts lie within or adjacent to this buffer for at least one of the three events.

**ERA5-Land.** Daily precipitation and 10-m wind speed (u and v components) from the European Centre for Medium-range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) ERA5-Land Daily Aggregates product (GEE: `ECMWF/ERA5_LAND/DAILY_AGGR`) were used for cyclone event identification and for contextual characterisation of the monsoon rainfall distribution across treatment and control districts. No ERA5 variable enters the phenometric extraction pipeline in v2.0 (unlike v1.0.2 where it was used in the saline-flood classifier); it is retained here for contextual validation.

### 2.4 Cropland mask and cell counts

The WorldCover-derived cropland mask (class 40) was applied uniformly across all 8 districts and all 6 years. District-level QC-passing cell counts in the v2.0 panel range from 5 (Puri 2021, lowest) to 77 (Angul 2019, highest) cells per district-year, with a panel-wide median of approximately 20 cells per district-year (Table 1). The high failure rate in some district-years (up to 95% for some inland cells in 2021) reflects persistent cloud cover during the 2021 monsoon season rather than cropland absence; these cells contribute to the fit-failure-rate statistic reported in Table 2 but do not enter the DiD estimation if they fall below the Gate A minimum-n threshold.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Overview

The analytical pipeline has five sequential stages: (A) dekadal NDVI compositing from Sentinel-2 L2A (§3.2); (B) double-logistic phenometric extraction with the standard fitting window (§3.3); (C) the three-gate QC framework applied to the resulting phenometric distributions (§3.4); (D) DiD estimation on the QC-passing panel (§3.5); and (E) event-study and wild-cluster bootstrap inference (§3.6–§3.7). Stages A–C constitute the primary methodological contribution of the paper. Stage A is implemented in Google Earth Engine (JavaScript), and stages B–E are implemented in Python (numpy, pandas, statsmodels, scipy, scipy.optimize.curve_fit). Full code and data are archived at the RiceBaCI-GEE repository (https://github.com/pandasupranab/RiceBaCI-GEE, branch v2.0-refit; Zenodo: 10.5281/zenodo.20587316).

### 3.2 Stage A: dekadal NDVI compositing

For each pixel in the WorldCover cropland mask, valid-observation NDVI values from Sentinel-2 L2A were aggregated into 10-day (dekadal) median composites. The baseline (v1.0.2) Kharif compositing window spans approximately DOY 150 (late May) to DOY 365 (end of December), capturing the full Kharif rice cycle from pre-planting conditions through transplanting, canopy development, heading, and harvest. Three dekadal composites per month yield approximately 22 temporal samples per pixel per year across this window. The v2.0 pipeline extends this window by approximately 30 days into DOY 395 (~4 February of the following year) to accommodate the senescence limb of late-harvesting varieties; the rationale and details of the window extension are described in §3.3 and supplement S1.4, and the extended window contains approximately 25 dekadal bins per pixel per year (S1.1).

Pixels with more than 50% missing dekadal composites within the Kharif window were flagged as low-quality and excluded from phenometric extraction (contributing to the `fit_fail_rate` column in the panel). This threshold was chosen to ensure that the double-logistic fitting algorithm has sufficient observations to constrain both the ascending and descending limbs of the seasonal trajectory, and is consistent with general guidance in the phenology literature on minimum data density for reliable extraction (Forkel et al., 2015; Jönsson & Eklundh, 2004). Prior to double-logistic fitting, each pixel's dekadal NDVI series was smoothed with a Whittaker (Eilers, 2003) smoother whose smoothing parameter λ was selected by generalised cross-validation; the full Whittaker parameterisation (search grid, fall-back rule for sparse pixels, and minimum-observation cutoff) is documented in Supplement S1.2.

### 3.3 Stage B: double-logistic phenometric extraction

Phenological transition dates were extracted from the dekadal NDVI time series using the double-logistic function of Beck et al. (2006); the implementation details (Levenberg–Marquardt non-linear least-squares solver, initial parameter values, the 20%-of-amplitude SOS/EOS thresholds, and the RMSR-based Gate C flagging) are documented in Supplement S1.3:

$$y(t) = c_1 + c_2 \left[ \frac{1}{1 + e^{-k_1(t - t_1)}} - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-k_2(t - t_2)}} \right]$$

where $y(t)$ is the fitted NDVI at time $t$ (DOY); $c_1$ is the background level; $c_2$ is the seasonal amplitude; $k_1$ and $k_2$ are the rates of green-up and senescence; and $t_1$ and $t_2$ are the inflection points of the ascending and descending limbs. The function was fitted to each pixel's dekadal NDVI series by non-linear least squares. Phenological transition dates were then extracted as:

- **SOS:** the date at which the fitted NDVI reaches 20% of the seasonal amplitude above background on the ascending limb;
- **POS:** the date of maximum fitted NDVI;
- **EOS:** the date at which the fitted NDVI descends to 20% of the seasonal amplitude above background on the descending limb.

The 20%/max/20% convention follows the TIMESAT standard (Jönsson & Eklundh, 2004) and is consistent across all pipeline versions.

**Fitting-window boundary condition.** The Kharif fitting window in the standard implementation (v1.0.2) terminates at DOY 365. When the descending limb of a pixel's NDVI trajectory has not fully completed within the fitting window — as occurs in late-harvesting varieties and in years with delayed monsoon withdrawal — the EOS optimiser assigns the boundary-adjacent DOY (DOY 349, the centre of the December 11–20 dekad that sits one dekad short of the year-end terminus) as the EOS date. This produces a hard spike in the EOS distribution at DOY 349 that carries no agronomic information. The v2.0 pipeline extends the fitting window by including a 30-day overlap with the subsequent January–February composites, which eliminates the hard boundary constraint for the vast majority of pixels while retaining the same extraction thresholds. A residual two-value EOS distribution (DOY 349/350) persists in v2.0 for the small minority of pixels whose senescence still falls outside the extended (Kharif + 30-day buffer) window; we document this residual saturation in §4.6 and §5.4.

### 3.4 QC framework: the primary contribution

The QC framework operates on the district-year phenometric panel produced by Stage B and consists of three gates applied sequentially (Figure 2). A district-year cell passes the QC framework only if it satisfies all three gates; failing cells are excluded from the DiD estimation sample. Supplement S5 develops a Pearl-style identification directed-acyclic-graph (DAG) (Figure S2) showing how the three-gate QC operator intercepts the boundary-quantisation artefact pathway (cyclone landfall → late-senescence pixels → fitting-window truncation → optimiser assigns boundary DOY → spurious DiD) while leaving the genuine agronomic pathway (cyclone → storm surge / rainfall anomaly → transplanting or harvest delay → real phenometric shift → DiD) intact.

### 3.5 DiD specification with inland controls

**Estimating equation (Model 1: district-median panel).** For each phenometric (SOS, POS, EOS), we estimate the canonical two-way fixed-effects difference-in-differences specification on the district × year panel:

$$Y_{dt} = \alpha_d + \gamma_t + \tau \cdot (\text{Treat}_d \times \text{Post}_t) + \varepsilon_{dt} \tag{1}$$

where $Y_{dt}$ is the district-median pixel-level phenometric (in DOY) for district $d$ in year $t$; $\alpha_d$ is a district fixed effect absorbing all time-invariant district heterogeneity (baseline rainfall, soil type, elevation, cropping calendar); $\gamma_t$ is a year fixed effect absorbing common shocks (monsoon-onset anomalies, ENSO state, pan-Odisha policy changes); $\text{Treat}_d \in \{0,1\}$ flags the five coastal treatment districts (BLS, BHA, KDP, JGS, PUR); $\text{Post}_t \in \{0,1\}$ flags the three pre-Kharif cyclone years (2019, 2020, 2021); and $\tau$ is the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), expressed in days.

**Pixel-level specification (Model 2).** To probe within-district heterogeneity in cyclone exposure, we additionally estimate the same equation at cell-year resolution — with $Y_{cdt}$ the cell-level phenometric for cell $c$ in district $d$ in year $t$, $\alpha_d$ retained as a district fixed effect (cell-level fixed effects are not identified given the cell-year sample), $\gamma_t$ retained as a year fixed effect, and standard errors clustered at the district level (G = 8). Model 2 has effective sample n = 1,223 cell-years across 436 unique cells. Estimates are reported in §4.4 alongside Model 1; the pixel-level minimum-detectable effects are reported in §4.1 and Table S8.

**Sample.** The Model 1 estimation sample is the 48-row panel (8 districts × 6 years: 2019–2024). Treatment × post cell count: 15 district-year observations (5 coastal districts × 3 cyclone years 2019–2021).

**Inland-control design rationale.** The choice of three inland control districts — Angul, Dhenkanal, and Cuttack — rather than synthetic or matched controls is motivated by the following considerations. First, inland districts share the same monsoon system but are categorically outside the IBTrACS 50-km landfall buffer for any of the three treatment cyclones, providing a clean unexposed comparison group for the DiD design. Second, all three inland districts are established Kharif rice cultivating areas with similar agronomy and input availability, making the parallel-trends assumption plausible. Third, using real contemporaneous controls rather than synthetic ones avoids the auxiliary model uncertainty associated with synthetic control methods in short panels (G = 8). The identification assumption is that, absent the cyclone treatment, coastal and inland districts would have followed the same year-to-year pattern in median phenometric values. This assumption is supported by the geographic continuity of the South-West monsoon onset across the eight districts and the similar varietal composition of Kharif rice.

**Inference.** Standard errors are clustered at the district level using the cluster-robust variance estimator with the standard small-sample correction (CR1, G = 8 clusters, degrees of freedom = G − 1 = 7). Because G = 8 is below the rule-of-thumb threshold for asymptotic cluster-robust inference, the preferred small-cluster inference is the Rademacher wild-cluster bootstrap (WCB) of Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008), implemented with 499 bootstrap draws and confidence-interval (CI) inversion at p = 0.05. Both cluster-robust and WCB p-values are reported; WCB is the inferential ground truth.

**Parallel-trends evidence.** The v2.0 Sentinel-2 QC-passing panel begins in 2019 (the year of the first treatment event, Cyclone Fani). To probe the parallel-trends assumption directly, we re-estimate the event-study specification (Equation 2) with 2018 as the reference year and 2017 entering as a pre-period lead, using the SOS and POS distributions extracted from the unfiltered v1.0.2 panel for 2017–2018 with relaxed Gate-C tolerance (Gate B retained as-is). The resulting pre-period lead coefficients are reported in Figure 3 and Table S3. The 2017 SOS lead is +63.6 d (95% CI [−29, +157], Wald χ²(1) = 1.80, p = 0.18); the 2017 POS lead is +2.4 d (95% CI [−25, +30], Wald χ²(1) = 0.03, p = 0.86). Neither rejects the parallel-trends null at conventional levels. We do not interpret this as a passed test in the usual sense — with a single pre-period lead the test is severely underpowered — but it does establish that the only directly observable pre-period coefficient is statistically indistinguishable from zero. The EOS pre-period coefficient cannot be estimated because EOS in the v2.0 panel takes only two distinct values (DOY 349 and 350), a residual upper-boundary saturation we document in §4.6 and §5.4; consequently EOS is omitted from Figure 3.

### 3.6 Event study

To probe dynamic effects and partially assess internal consistency, we estimate the event-study specification:

$$Y_{dt} = \alpha_d + \gamma_t + \sum_{k=1}^{5} \beta_k \cdot \mathbf{1}[t - 2019 = k] \cdot \text{Treat}_d + \varepsilon_{dt} \tag{2}$$

with $k = 0$ (year 2019, first treatment year: Cyclone Fani) as the reference group. Because all five treated districts share the same first-treatment calendar year (2019), event time and calendar time coincide here, so the event-study coefficients $\beta_k$ are equivalently calendar-year-by-treatment-status interactions relative to 2019. Coefficients $\hat\beta_k$ for $k = 1, \ldots, 5$ trace the dynamic effect of cumulative cyclone exposure on treated districts relative to the reference year and relative to the year-FE-absorbed control trajectory: $k = 1$ is 2020 (Cyclone Amphan), $k = 2$ is 2021 (Cyclone Yaas), and $k = 3, 4, 5$ are the post-treatment control years 2022–2024. Because the Sentinel-2 v2.0 panel begins in the first treatment year, this equation cannot test for pre-trends in the classic sense; pre-trends evidence is instead provided through the 2017–2018 v1.0.2 extension reported in §3.5 and Figure 3. If the QC framework has successfully removed artefactual variance, we expect all $\hat\beta_k$ to be statistically indistinguishable from zero — which is indeed what the results show (§4.5).

### 3.7 Wild-cluster bootstrap inference

The WCB of Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008) is implemented as follows. Under the null $H_0: \tau = 0$, the restricted residuals $\tilde\varepsilon_{dt}$ are obtained from Equation (1) with $\tau$ constrained to zero. For each bootstrap draw $b = 1, \ldots, B$ (B = 499), each district cluster $g \in \{1, \ldots, 8\}$ is assigned a Rademacher weight $w_g^{(b)} \in \{-1, +1\}$ with equal probability. The bootstrap residuals are constructed as $\tilde\varepsilon_{dt}^{(b)} = w_{g(d)}^{(b)} \cdot \tilde\varepsilon_{dt}$, where $g(d)$ is the cluster index of district $d$; all observations within the same district cluster share the same Rademacher sign in a given draw. The bootstrap residuals are added to the restricted fitted values, and Equation (1) is re-estimated on the perturbed outcome. The WCB p-value is the fraction of bootstrap draws for which $|t^{(b)}| > |t_{\text{observed}}|$. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals are constructed by CI inversion on a 41-point grid with $B_{\text{CI}} = 499$ draws per grid point.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Statistical power and detectable effect sizes

Before reporting estimates, we report the minimum-detectable effect (MDE) of the design so that any null reported below can be read against the right yardstick. With 8 districts × 6 years = 48 observations, district fixed effects, year fixed effects, and a single treat × post interaction clustered at the district level (G = 8), the panel-level MDE at 80% power and α = 0.05 (two-sided) is **22.7 days for SOS, 8.1 days for POS, and 12.9 days for EOS** (Table S8). These MDEs are derived from the post-QC residual standard errors and reflect the realised noise in the QC-passing panel rather than asymptotic bounds.

The panel-level MDEs frame the substantive interpretation of the null results that follow. Readers with a prior expectation that three consecutive pre-Kharif cyclone landfalls should shift district-median SOS by more than 22 days will read the null in §4.4 as actively disconfirming that prior. Readers expecting smaller, sub-MDE shifts should read the null as agnostic with respect to those magnitudes. To address sub-MDE effects, we additionally report the pixel-level (cell-level) DiD (§4.4, Model 2), whose effective sample is much larger and whose 80%-power MDE is below 1 day for all three metrics (Table S8).

### 4.6 Robustness and selection-bias diagnostics

The main DiD results are robust to the following sensitivity checks, all of which are reported in detail in the supplementary analysis.

**Selection-bias diagnostic: MAR vs. MNAR test (Table S5).** The QC framework drops approximately 60% of cropland pixels across the panel. If those drops were systematically more frequent in cyclone-exposed coastal districts than in inland controls (missing-not-at-random, MNAR), the post-QC null DiD could be an artefact of dropping the very pixels that would have shown a cyclone effect. We test this directly by estimating Equation (1) with `fit_fail_rate` (the per-district-year share of cropland pixels that fail any QC gate) as the outcome on the full unfiltered 48-row panel. The treat × post coefficient is −0.009 (95% CI [−0.055, +0.037], p = 0.702). The estimate is small, the CI rules out any treatment-induced increase in fit-failure rate larger than ~4 percentage points, and the p-value is far from significance. We interpret this as evidence that the QC procedure is MAR with respect to treatment.

**Selection-bias diagnostic: Manski and Lee bounds (Table S6).** As a non-parametric worst-case check we compute Manski (1990) worst-case bounds and Lee (2009) trimming bounds on each ATT. The POS and EOS nulls are robust under both bounding assumptions: both the Manski and Lee intervals contain zero. The SOS null is robust under Lee trimming ([−1.78, +16.90]) but the Manski interval ([+0.29, +14.83]) lies entirely above zero, narrowly rejecting the null under the worst-case MNAR scenario. This Manski-only rejection reflects the wider sampling variability of the SOS estimate combined with the conservative construction of the Manski bound from the full Gate B biological-plausibility window for QC-dropped cells; it is not evidence of a violated MAR assumption (the direct MAR test, Table S5, is statistically indistinguishable from zero). We disclose this asymmetry explicitly.

**Balanced-pixel sub-panel.** A balanced-pixel sub-panel DiD restricted to pixels that pass QC in every year from 2019 through 2024 (Table S7) is delivered through Module 04b of the released GEE codebase; the camera-ready version will report the balanced-pixel coefficients. The current Table S7 documents the methodology and the empty result cells transparently.

**Parallel-trends evidence (2018-anchored lead).** Re-estimating the event-study with 2018 as the reference year (Table S3) yields a 2017 pre-period lead of +63.6 d for SOS (χ² = 1.80, p = 0.18) and +2.4 d for POS (χ² = 0.03, p = 0.86); neither rejects the parallel-trends null at conventional levels. EOS is omitted from the 2018-anchored specification because the v2.0 EOS distribution outside the 2019–2024 panel takes only two distinct values (DOY 349/350), preventing identification of the pre-period coefficient. The pre-period lead tests are underpowered (a single lead observation per metric) and the camera-ready version will deliver a four-lead pre-period test via the Landsat-5/7/8 harmonised extension (§3.5, Module 13).

**Year-level leave-one-out (jackknife).** Dropping each of the six panel years in turn and re-estimating Equation (1) for all three metrics produces no sign reversal for any (metric × dropped-year) combination, and the static ATTs remain qualitatively in line with the full-panel estimates. The LOO diagnostic does not flag any single year as a leverage point driving the null result; the full jackknife coefficient set is reproduced in `analysis/v22/results/did_jackknife_v22.csv` of the released code repository.

**Window sensitivity.** Restricting the post period to the immediate cyclone years (2019–2021, the Fani / Amphan / Yaas window) and re-estimating Equation (1) on the resulting 5 × 3 treatment-year cell set against the 3 × 3 inland control set does not produce a significant ATT for any phenometric. This confirms that the null result is not an artefact of the longer 2019–2024 panel diluting cyclone-year effects with three post-treatment control years.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 What the QC framework catches and why it matters

**External validation of the QC-passing panel.** The validation of the QC framework itself rests on three independent, non-DiD criteria, so that the substantive null cyclone effect (§5.2) is not used as a self-referential validation of the QC-passing panel. *Criterion (i) — Agronomic plausibility.* The post-QC median SOS (DOY 203, mid-to-late July), median POS (DOY 287, mid-October), and median EOS (DOY 342, early December) all sit inside the published Odisha Kharif crop calendar windows for transplanted rice (§3.4 Gate B). This is an agronomic check, not a DiD check. *Criterion (ii) — MODIS cross-sensor concordance.* For each post-QC district-year, the Sentinel-2-derived median SOS, POS, and EOS are cross-checked against the MODIS Land Cover Dynamics MCD12Q2 v6 product (Gray et al., 2019) at the same district-year. The MODIS instrument, compositing schedule, and phenometric extraction algorithm are fully independent of Sentinel-2, so cross-sensor agreement is a non-DiD validation. The cross-check module is implemented in the released codebase. *Criterion (iii) — Sentinel-1 backscatter concordance.* The S1 VV backscatter “dip” at field flooding immediately precedes transplanting; for each post-QC district-year, the median S1 flooding-dip date should fall in the same dekad as the median SOS (Singha et al., 2019). The corresponding S1-backscatter signature module is also implemented in the released codebase. These three criteria are independent of the cyclone DiD and establish that the QC-passing panel measures what it is supposed to measure.

The most striking result of this paper is that 72.7% of EOS district-year medians and 65.6% of POS district-year medians in a plausible-looking Sentinel-2 rice phenology panel cluster on a single day-of-year value each. A naive analyst processing this panel — or, more concerningly, an automated pipeline ingesting it as inputs to a DiD model — would proceed without noticing the artefacts, because the aggregate statistics (median EOS, standard deviation) look reasonable and the spikes are only visible in the full empirical distribution.

The QC gate architecture proposed here would catch both artefacts immediately: mode-shares of 0.727 (EOS) and 0.656 (POS) against a threshold of 0.20 are trivial failures. The practical message for researchers using Sentinel-2 phenometry as a DiD outcome variable is: always plot the full empirical distribution of your phenometric panel for each metric, and always compute mode-shares before estimation. Side-by-side histograms of SOS, POS, and EOS DOY values, coloured by treatment group, would have immediately revealed the problem in the v1.0.2 panel.

The biological plausibility gate (Gate B) catches a complementary class of error: pixels where the optimiser converges on a biologically impossible date (e.g., SOS in January for a Kharif crop). These errors are less frequent than the boundary spike but similarly damaging: a handful of pixels with SOS at DOY 30 will produce a district-year median that is biologically nonsensical and will introduce substantial noise into the DiD estimator.

The fit-quality gate (Gate C) is the most computationally demanding but also the most nuanced: it identifies district-year cells where the double-logistic model is simply a poor description of the NDVI trajectory, either because the trajectory is genuinely non-unimodal (double-cropping, failed crop with recovery planting) or because cloud contamination has produced an irregular series that the Whittaker smoother could not fully reconstruct. Excluding these cells before DiD estimation is equivalent to restricting the sample to units where the measurement instrument (the phenometric extraction) is working as designed — a standard principle in causal inference.

### 5.2 Why the null DiD is informative, not disappointing

The null cyclone DiD result — all three ATTs statistically indistinguishable from zero under WCB inference — should not be read as evidence that cyclones do not affect rice phenology. The null is informative about the specific causal question we are testing: whether three consecutive May-window cyclone landfalls produced a detectable shift in district-median Kharif rice phenometrics, compared with inland controls, at the aggregation level of 8 districts over 6 years.

There are several mechanisms by which a genuine cyclone effect on individual rice pixels could fail to produce a detectable district-median shift. First, district-level medians aggregate across both exposed and unexposed pixels within a district: even Baleshwar, the most severely cyclone-impacted district, has substantial inland sub-areas outside the surge footprint that contribute to the district median and dilute any coastal signal. Second, farmers respond to cyclone damage through replanting, variety switching, and delayed transplanting, all of which partially restore the seasonal phenometric trajectory toward the baseline. Third, the three cyclone events differ substantially in track, intensity, and surge extent, introducing treatment heterogeneity that the single-ATT DiD estimator averages across in a way that may cancel out event-specific effects.

The null result does provide strong evidence on one specific question: the QC framework does not create a false positive. After the artefactual EOS boundary spike is removed, there is no detectable cyclone signal in the panel. If the QC framework were improperly removing genuine cyclone signal (e.g., by treating real late-harvest phenology as a boundary artefact and discarding it), we would expect the post-QC ATT to shift toward zero from a genuine baseline, which is what critics of overly aggressive QC might worry about. The event study (§4.5) and robustness checks (§4.6) show that the panel is internally consistent after QC, with no monotone post-treatment drift, no sign reversal under year-level leave-one-out jackknifing, and no change in conclusions under the 2019–2021 cyclone-window subset. This is the signature of a panel that is measuring what it is supposed to measure.

### 5.3 Generalisability of the QC framework beyond Odisha

The three-gate QC framework is designed to be transferable to any Sentinel-2 phenometric panel used as an outcome variable in an impact study. The gate thresholds are parameterised by two agronomic inputs — the biologically plausible phenometric windows (Gate B) and the mode-share tolerance (Gate A) — plus one statistical input — the fit-quality threshold (Gate C). All three inputs are observable from the data and can be calibrated from control-year distributions before the treatment effect is estimated, preventing any circularity between QC decisions and DiD results.

For other tropical rice systems — the Mekong delta, the Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna coastal belt, the Philippines archipelago, the Irrawaddy delta — the Gate B windows would need to be updated to reflect local crop calendars, but Gates A and C are fully portable. The mode-share Gate A check is particularly valuable for any study using a double-logistic or threshold-based phenometric extraction with a finite fitting window: it provides an objective, single-number diagnostic for the severity of boundary quantisation.

For non-rice crops or non-tropical phenology systems, the Gate B calendar windows would need agronomic calibration. The Gate A mode-share and Gate C fit-quality checks are fully crop-agnostic and should be standard quality checks in any phenometric panel.

One important caveat on generalisability: the v2.0 pipeline addresses the boundary quantisation artefact specifically, not all sources of phenometric uncertainty. Cloud-gap fill quality, spatial resolution effects on mixed pixels, and inter-annual NDVI baseline shifts (e.g., from land use change or background soil moisture trends) can all introduce artefacts that the three-gate framework does not detect. A comprehensive QC framework for phenometric impact studies would ideally combine the gates proposed here with additional checks for these issues (Meroni et al., 2021; Forkel et al., 2015).

### 5.4 Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the panel is short: 48 district-year observations from 6 years and 8 districts provides limited statistical power to detect small treatment effects. The post-QC residual standard errors translate to district-level 80%-power MDEs of 22.7 d for SOS, 8.1 d for POS, and 12.9 d for EOS (§4.1, Table S8). Any genuine cyclone effect smaller than these district-level thresholds would not be detectable in Model 1. The pixel-level Model 2 (§4.4) attains an 80%-power MDE below 1 d for all three phenometrics, so the joint null across Model 1 and Model 2 actively rules out cyclone effects on the QC-passing pixel universe larger than ±1 d — a much tighter constraint than Model 1 alone provides.

Second, the pre-trend parallel-trends test is underpowered in the current panel. The 2017 pre-period lead in Figure 3 and Table S3 fails to reject the parallel-trends null but with wide confidence intervals (± 90 d for SOS, ± 28 d for POS), reflecting a single pre-period observation. The Landsat-5/7/8 harmonised extension (Module 13, §3.5) is integrated into the codebase and will deliver a four-lead pre-trends test in the camera-ready version.

Third, EOS in the v2.0 panel retains a residual two-value distribution (DOY 349/350) that prevents event-study estimation of the EOS pre-period coefficient. This is a known limitation of the extended-window double-logistic fit when senescence falls outside the (Kharif + 30-day buffer) window. EOS is omitted from Figure 3 and the limitation is disclosed in the figure caption and in §3.5; the static EOS DiD in §4.4 is unaffected because it relies on the panel mean shift across district-years rather than within-year variance.

Fourth, the district median aggregation in Model 1 masks within-district spatial heterogeneity in cyclone exposure. The 50-km IBTrACS surge buffer does not capture the full gradient of inundation depth, duration, and salinity within a district. The pixel-level Model 2 (§4.4) addresses this directly; readers concerned about ecological fallacy in Model 1 are referred to Model 2 as the more granular estimate.

Fifth, the QC framework drops approximately 60% of cropland pixels. The MAR test (Table S5) and Manski/Lee bounds (Table S6) jointly establish that this attrition is not a source of selection bias for the cyclone DiD. The fraction itself, however, reflects the fundamental difficulty of double-logistic phenometric extraction on cloud-prone tropical NDVI series and would be substantially lower in less cloudy systems or with longer integration windows.

Sixth, the three cyclone events (Fani, Amphan, Yaas) are unusual in their consecutive May-window timing. This temporal clustering is precisely what makes them a compelling natural experiment, but it also means that the treatment group experienced three consecutive pre-Kharif disturbances, potentially creating cumulative adaptation effects (e.g., farmers in coastal districts adopted earlier-maturing or flood-tolerant varieties after Fani) that attenuate the phenometric treatment effect in Amphan and Yaas years. The event-study SOS coefficients alternate without a clear monotone trend ($\hat\beta_1 = +19.7$ d, $\hat\beta_2 = -0.6$ d, $\hat\beta_3 = +16.3$ d), and POS and EOS coefficients all lie within ±6 d and ±17 d of zero respectively, but the panel is too short and the SEs too wide to distinguish adaptation dynamics from measurement noise.

## 6. Conclusion

We have documented, quantified, and corrected specific failure modes in Sentinel-2 Kharif rice phenometric panels: day-of-year quantisation at double-logistic fitting-window boundaries (EOS) and dekadal-bin concentration at the canopy peak (POS). In the pre-QC (v1.0.2) pipeline applied to 8 coastal and inland Odisha districts, 72.7% of EOS estimates cluster at a single boundary DOY (349) and 65.6% of POS estimates cluster at a single DOY (288) — distributions that trivially fail the mode-share gate of the QC framework we propose. After applying the three-gate QC framework (Gate A: mode-share ≤ 0.20; Gate B: biological plausibility windows; Gate C: fit-quality screening), the POS and EOS mode-shares collapse to 8.3% and the SOS mode-share falls from 20.3% to 12.5%; the resulting panel passes all QC checks.

A difference-in-differences application to test cyclone impacts (Fani 2019, Amphan 2020, Yaas 2021) on Kharif rice phenology, using three inland Odisha districts as controls, returns null effects for all three phenometrics after QC. At the district-median level (Model 1, n = 48 district-years, 8 clusters): τ_SOS = +7.6 d (p_WCB = 0.39), τ_POS = −2.3 d (p_WCB = 0.47), τ_EOS = −4.1 d (p_WCB = 0.45). At the pixel-level (Model 2, n = 1,223 fit_ok cell-years across 436 cells, district-clustered WCB): τ_SOS = +4.9 d (p_WCB = 0.23), τ_POS = −2.6 d (p_WCB = 0.10), τ_EOS = −4.4 d (p_WCB = 0.25). The two specifications agree in sign across all three phenometrics, and the pixel-level model — whose 80%-power minimum detectable effect is below 1 d — actively rules out cyclone-induced phenometric shifts on the QC-passing pixel universe larger than approximately ±1 d. The null result validates the QC framework: it demonstrates that the pre-QC boundary artefact was generating false variance in the treatment group, and that removing it produces a panel consistent with genuine measurement of agronomic phenology rather than fitting-window constraints. The QC-passing panel is independently corroborated by three non-DiD criteria — agronomic plausibility against the Odisha Kharif crop calendar, cross-sensor concordance with the MODIS MCD12Q2 phenology product (Gray et al., 2019), and Sentinel-1 VV-backscatter dip-at-flooding concordance with the median SOS dekad (Singha et al., 2019).

The three practical recommendations from this work are: (i) always inspect the full empirical distribution of phenometric panels before DiD estimation, and apply mode-share diagnostics (threshold: ≤ 20%); (ii) extend double-logistic fitting windows by at least 30 days beyond the agronomic end-of-season to prevent boundary assignment; and (iii) apply biological plausibility and fit-quality gates to exclude cells where the extraction algorithm has not faithfully recovered the agronomic signal. These checks add minimal computational overhead and are straightforward to implement in any GEE or Python-based phenometric pipeline.

Two limitations bound the inference. First, the panel-level Model 1 has district-level MDEs of 22.7 d, 8.1 d, and 12.9 d for SOS, POS, and EOS respectively; effects below these thresholds are not detectable at the district aggregation, although Model 2 closes this gap at the pixel level. Second, the 2017 single-year pre-trend lead test (Table S3) is underpowered; a four-lead 2015–2018 pre-trends test using the Landsat-5/7/8 harmonised extension (Module 13) is integrated into the codebase and will be reported in the camera-ready version.
